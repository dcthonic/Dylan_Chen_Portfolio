Project 03 · Case Study

24/7 Shift
Roster Generator

I turned a manual, rule-heavy 24/7 shift roster into a tool that builds a fair schedule from three input sheets. It exists as an Excel workbook that runs a Python solver, and as a Google Sheets version that runs entirely in Apps Script. Both check their own output against every rule and say plainly when the rules cannot be met.

The Problem

The roster covers AM, PM and Graveyard shifts and had to satisfy several rules at once. Doing it by hand meant juggling dozens of constraints, and every change to the rules or absences forced a rebuild.

  • Everyone works 5 of 7 days in the AM week, 5 of 7 in the PM week and 2 of 7 in the Graveyard week.
  • At least 15 on duty for AM and PM and 5 for Graveyard, with at least 3 '#' personnel on AM and PM and 1 on Graveyard.
  • Nobody is scheduled during medical leave, course, off or other leave; long absences remove a person from that week.
  • Headcount, '#' names and each of the four sections should be level day to day, with one permanent-AM exception.
  • A points system should show who contributes most without penalising people who were absent.

My Role

I owned the problem from the first rule to the final tool: I defined the requirements, tested each version of the output against the real rules, and decided what a fair outcome meant. I worked with an AI assistant for code generation, and used my own review to check results and steer each iteration.

How It Works

The solver treats the roster as a mixed-integer optimisation problem: one yes/no variable for every person, shift type and day, plus helper variables that measure how uneven the days, sections and points are.

  • Hard rules cannot be broken: no shifts on absence dates, required days per person, minimum headcount and minimum '#' names.
  • Goals are weighted penalties: level daily headcount first, then '#' names, weekend limits and section balance, with points balance as a tie-breaker.
  • Fairness is each person's points as a percentage of a fair-share target scaled to the shifts they were required to work, so absence never lowers a score.
A pre-check runs before solving. If the rules cannot be met, for example because too few people are available on a given day, the tool stops and names the day and the shortage instead of producing a bad roster.

Build Progress

01–02

First roster and hardening the rules

Generated a roster in Excel with linked points tracking. Reviewing the rules exposed an impossible one: a cap of 4 '#' names per day allows 28 shifts, but six '#' people working five days each need 30. I split rules into hard rules and goals instead.

03

Redesigning fairness

Early weekend multipliers penalised people who were away. I moved to a burden-points system weighting evenings and nights, with a weekend premium, a recovery debit after nights and a penalty for back-to-back nights, measured against a fair-share target.

04–06

Exact mode, automation and refinements

Added an EXACT mode that staffs only the minimum, packaged the logic with an Excel macro and template, rewrote it for Google Sheets, then added a weekend limit, dated sheet names, auto-hiding of old rosters and a variation setting.

07–08

Cross-cycle tracking and Enhanced Graveyard

Replaced a monthly ranking idea with one persistent Points Log that only counts cycles a member appears in. Added an in-place Enhanced Graveyard action that fills to 10 staff per day with 2–3 '#' members, favouring members below 100%.

09–11

Usability, overrides and sharing

Added soft preferred days off, absence-based quota reductions, per-member workday overrides (0–7), staged Graveyard planning, and a stable Current Roster tab so an external IMPORTRANGE keeps receiving new cycles.

One finding was a limit rather than a result: with 15 people needed each weekend day and about 24 available, some people must work both weekend days. The tool reports how many are unavoidable, so the weekend rule became a minimised soft limit rather than a hard promise.

Problems Solved

  • An impossible rule. Counting shifts showed the '#' cap could never be met, so I fixed the rule instead of the schedule.
  • A fairness measure that punished absence. A fair-share target removed the penalty and made comparison meaningful.
  • A bug caught by verification. A cross-check showed one score out of line: the optimiser had charged a night-shift penalty to consecutive AM shifts for the permanent-AM exception. I fixed the model and re-ran before using any results.
  • A solver that could not prove its best. Tight fairness targets ran out of time, so I ordered the priorities and recorded where results were good but not proven optimal.
  • Safe variation. Variation only breaks ties between equally good rosters, so it cannot loosen the rules.
  • A #VALUE! error in the shared roster tab. Traced to a missing ARRAYFORMULA wrapper and fixed.

How I Checked My Work

I did not trust any single result. Every roster was re-checked by a separate script that recomputed headcounts, section differences, absences and every person's points hour by hour from the shift times. Workbooks were recalculated to confirm zero formula errors. For the Google version, I ran the exact script against a stand-in for Google's optimiser and confirmed it produced the same rosters as the Python version.

Limitations & Next Steps

Status as of 29 October 2026: recent updates (workday overrides, staged Graveyard planning, the stable Current Roster tab) are implemented in source but still need deploying and checking in the live Google Sheet.

  • End-to-end runs have ranged from about 20 to 300 seconds depending on configuration; I plan to benchmark solver time separately from sheet I/O.
  • Points weights are judgement-based and adjustable, not derived from measured fatigue data.
  • The Excel macro needs desktop Excel on Windows; Excel for the web cannot run it.
  • Next: confirm the IMPORTRANGE flow live, validate leave, overrides and Enhanced Graveyard at full size, and add a minimum rest time between shifts.

What This Shows

I can take an ambiguous, people-centred problem, turn it into a precise model, build a tool that other people can run, and prove that it works. The weakest parts of the project were the places I checked hardest, which is why the limits above are stated plainly.
